\begin{abstract}
We present a systematic Pareto replication study of mixed-precision
attention on the NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5080 (Blackwell, sm\_120a)---a
mid-tier consumer GPU underrepresented in published kernel-level
attention work. Five hand-rolled kernels spanning FP32 to NVFP4 share a
single algorithmic skeleton (online softmax, single-kernel fusion,
register-resident accumulator from V3 onward), so each step in the
optimization ladder isolates one variable. We report three findings:
(i)~adding online softmax and fusion reduces peak HBM at sequence
length 8192 by $17\times$, exactly matching the PyTorch SDPA baseline
without changing precision; (ii)~hand-rolled FP8 (V3) closes
approximately half of the SDPA latency gap, confirming that custom
kernels using a precision the vendor library does not engage---SDPA on
this PyTorch~2.9 build dispatches only to a math fallback---expose a
real hardware lever; and (iii)~NVFP4 (V4) with software microscaling on
\texttt{kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32} is $\approx 30\%$ \emph{slower} than V3,
because the per-mma microscaling overhead exceeds the FP4 throughput
advantage at the consumer-Blackwell software stack we target. The
hardware-microscaled \texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} family would plausibly
invert this result but is incompatible with the row-major-SMEM idiom we
share with V3, and we treat it as documented future work. Our open
artifact is reproducible on a single 16~GB consumer GPU and includes
full benchmark drivers, kernels, and figure regeneration.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
attention, mixed precision, FP8, NVFP4, FlashAttention, GPU kernels,
Tensor Cores, consumer Blackwell, sm\_120a, reproducibility

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Scaled dot-product attention is the dominant compute and memory cost in
contemporary transformer inference. Its quadratic dependence on sequence
length has motivated a decade of kernel-level optimization---tiling,
fusion, online softmax, and progressively narrower numerical
precisions---that has compressed both wall-clock latency and high-
bandwidth-memory (HBM) footprint by more than an order of magnitude on
datacenter GPUs.

The two largest individual contributions to this trajectory are the
\emph{FlashAttention} family~\cite{dao2022flashattention,dao2024flashattention2,shah2024flashattention3},
which replaces the materialized $N \times N$ attention matrix with a
single fused, tile-streaming kernel, and the \emph{mixed-precision
attention} literature~\cite{micikevicius2022fp8,zhang2025sageattention3},
which exploits 5\textsuperscript{th}-generation Tensor Cores' native FP8
and FP4 arithmetic. Each of these ideas has been independently validated
on Hopper (H100) and on the top-of-stack Blackwell datacenter parts
(B100/B200, sm\_100a) and on the flagship consumer Blackwell card
RTX~5090 (sm\_120a)~\cite{zhang2025sageattention3}.

A gap remains. The bulk of academic and industrial interest in
\emph{consumer} Blackwell has fixated on the RTX~5090. The mid-tier
RTX~5080 (sm\_120a, 84 SMs, 16~GB GDDR7, $\approx$960~GB/s) is the actual
practical inference target for individual researchers, small labs, and
edge-deployed serving stacks; it shares a compute capability with the
5090 but has materially different SM count, L2 capacity, and memory
bandwidth, and---as we document---a different software-stack story for
GPU-attention dispatch on Windows. Whether the FlashAttention algorithmic
gains, FP8 throughput benefits, and FP4 microscaling promises
\emph{transfer} to this constrained-but-realistic platform is unstudied.

\paragraph*{Contribution} We perform a systematic Pareto replication
study of mixed-precision attention on the RTX~5080 (Blackwell, sm\_120a)
across five hand-rolled kernels spanning FP32 to NVFP4, all compiled to
machine code on the same toolchain (CUDA~12.8, PyTorch~2.9.1, sm\_120a)
and benchmarked against pinned PyTorch SDPA backends. The kernels share
a single algorithmic skeleton---online softmax with single-kernel
fusion---so each step in the precision and optimization ladder isolates
exactly one variable. Specifically:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{V0 (FP32 naive):} correctness oracle, full $O(N^2)$
        materialization in HBM.
  \item \textbf{V1 (FP16 tiled):} hand-rolled WMMA, but still
        materializes the score and probability matrices in HBM. Isolates
        \emph{tiling and Tensor-Core engagement} from algorithmic
        fusion.
  \item \textbf{V2 (FP16 fused):} FlashAttention-style online softmax,
        single-kernel fusion. Isolates \emph{algorithmic fusion's}
        contribution.
  \item \textbf{V3 (FP8, E4M3):} hand-rolled \texttt{mma.sync m16n8k32}
        inline PTX with per-tile FP8 scaling and a register-resident
        output accumulator. Isolates \emph{narrower-precision Tensor
        Cores} on consumer Blackwell, where the CUTLASS sm\_120a FMHA
        reference does not exist as of v4.4.2~\cite{nvidia2024cutlass}.
  \item \textbf{V4 (NVFP4, E2M1):} per-row, per-K-block (block size 32)
        software microscaling on the
        \texttt{kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32.e2m1.e2m1.f32} mma.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Findings} Three results stand out, two confirmatory and one
negative:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Memory wall breaks at V2.} Adding online softmax and
        fusion reduces peak HBM at sequence length 8192 from 2176~MB to
        128~MB---a $17\times$ reduction that exactly matches the SDPA
        baseline---without any precision change.
  \item \textbf{V3 closes a measurable share of the SDPA latency gap
        with FP8.} V3 is $2.4\times$ faster than V2 and reaches roughly
        half of SDPA's throughput, even though SDPA on this PyTorch
        build dispatches only to a math fallback (Section~\ref{sec:exp}).
        Custom kernels using a precision the vendor library does not
        engage expose a real hardware lever.
  \item \textbf{V4 loses to V3 by $\approx 30\%$.} On consumer Blackwell,
        software microscaling overhead dominates the FP4 throughput
        advantage at the \texttt{mma.sync} call granularity. The
        hardware-microscaled \texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} family would
        plausibly invert this result, but its CuTe scattered-fragment
        layout is incompatible with the row-major SMEM idiom used by V3.
        We document this as a deferred-work design lever.
\end{enumerate}

The negative V4 result is, to our knowledge, the first quantitative
evidence that low-precision attention does not strictly win on consumer
Blackwell hardware, in contrast to the purely positive findings reported
on H100 and RTX~5090. Together with the SDPA-dispatch finding, this
argues for treating each Blackwell tier as a distinct study target rather
than extrapolating from a single hardware reference.

\paragraph*{Roadmap} Section~\ref{sec:bg} reviews the algorithmic and
hardware primitives the five variants compose. Section~\ref{sec:related}
positions our work against existing FlashAttention and FP4-attention
literature. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes each variant's design
rationale and the lessons forced by sm\_120a-specific tooling.
Section~\ref{sec:exp} fixes our experimental protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the Pareto frontier and headline
numbers. Sections~\ref{sec:disc} and~\ref{sec:limits} discuss
implications and limitations. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} is the
reproducibility checklist.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:bg}

\paragraph*{Scaled dot-product attention} For queries $Q \in
\mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, keys $K \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, and
values $V \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, the standard formulation is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\, Q K^\top\right) V,
\label{eq:attn}
\end{equation}
where the softmax is row-wise over the score matrix $S = QK^\top \in
\mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Materializing $S$
costs $O(N^2)$ in HBM, which is the central problem the FlashAttention
family attacks.

\paragraph*{IO-aware tiling and online softmax} FlashAttention
\cite{dao2022flashattention} streams over $K$ and $V$ in tiles, computes
partial scores and partial probabilities tile-by-tile, and maintains a
\emph{running} per-row maximum $m$ and partition sum $\ell$ via the
recurrence
\begin{align}
  m^{(t)}      &= \max(m^{(t-1)},\, \max_j S_{:, j}^{(t)}), \\
  \alpha^{(t)} &= \exp(m^{(t-1)} - m^{(t)}), \\
  \ell^{(t)}   &= \alpha^{(t)} \ell^{(t-1)} + \textstyle\sum_j \exp(S_{:, j}^{(t)} - m^{(t)}), \\
  O^{(t)}      &= \alpha^{(t)} O^{(t-1)} + \tilde P^{(t)} V^{(t)},
\end{align}
where $\tilde P^{(t)}_{ij} = \exp(S^{(t)}_{ij} - m^{(t)})$ is the
unnormalized tile probability and $O$ the unnormalized output. A single
epilogue divides by $\ell^{(N/B_C)}$ to recover the canonical attention.
The recurrence is numerically stable, exact, and avoids storing $S$ or
$P$ in HBM. We adopt this kernel structure in V2--V4.

Two corner cases of the recurrence are not stated in the FlashAttention
algorithm box but are required for correctness: the \emph{first
iteration}, where $m^{(0)} = -\infty$ and $\ell^{(0)} = 0$, and the
\emph{fully-masked} case (causal masking past the diagonal, padded rows)
where $m^{(t)}$ remains $-\infty$ and the naive $\alpha^{(t)} =
\exp(m^{(t-1)} - m^{(t)})$ produces NaN. Our V2 kernel guards both:
$\alpha^{(t)} = 1$ when $m^{(t)} = -\infty$, and $\tilde P$ is zeroed on
masked elements before the row-sum.

\paragraph*{Mixed-precision Tensor Cores on Blackwell} 5\textsuperscript{th}-
generation Tensor Cores expose the synchronous matrix-multiply-accumulate
\texttt{mma.sync} family. On consumer Blackwell (sm\_120a) the
instructions relevant to attention are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{m16n8k16.row.col.f32.f16.f16.f32}: the FP16 tensor-core
        mma engaged by V1 and V2 via \texttt{nvcuda::wmma}~\cite{nvidia2024ptxisa}.
        SASS mnemonic \texttt{HMMA.16816.F32}.
  \item \texttt{m16n8k32.row.col.f32.e4m3.e4m3.f32}: FP8 (E4M3) sync
        mma; used by V3 via inline PTX. SASS mnemonic on sm\_120 is
        \texttt{QMMA.16832.F32.E4M3.E4M3}.
  \item \texttt{kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32.row.col.f32.e2m1.e2m1.f32}: FP4
        (E2M1) sync mma; used by V4. The \texttt{kind::f8f6f4} feature
        requires the architecture-accelerated \texttt{sm\_120a} target;
        ptxas rejects it on plain \texttt{sm\_120}. SASS mnemonic
        \texttt{QMMA.16832.F32.E2M1.E2M1}.
\end{itemize}
Per-thread fragment layouts for these mmas are documented in PTX ISA
\S9.7.13.5~\cite{nvidia2024ptxisa} and follow a consistent pattern: with
$g = \mathrm{laneID} \gg 2$ and $t = \mathrm{laneID} \,\&\, 3$, the A
fragment holds row pairs $(g, g+8)$ at 4-element column groups indexed
by $t$. This idiom maps directly to row-major shared-memory tiles via
\texttt{reinterpret\_cast<uint32\_t*>} accesses with no
\texttt{ldmatrix} required.

\paragraph*{Hardware microscaling and \texttt{mxf4nvf4}} An additional
NVFP4 instruction family, \texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64}, internalizes a
per-16-element scale factor at the hardware level. CUTLASS exposes the
family via \texttt{cute::SM120\_16x8x64\_TN}~\cite{nvidia2024cutlass},
but its A and B layouts use \emph{scattered} per-thread fragments
incompatible with the consecutive-byte row-major SMEM access pattern
V1--V3 use; engaging the family requires \texttt{ldmatrix}-style
swizzled SMEM and a CUTLASS \texttt{CollectiveBuilder}-mediated load.
We discuss the consequences in Section~\ref{sec:disc} and treat the
\texttt{mxf4nvf4} path as deferred work.

\paragraph*{FP8 and FP4 numeric envelopes} E4M3 (4 exponent bits, 3
mantissa bits) provides $\approx 16$ representable positive values and
maximum representable magnitude 448; E2M1 (2 exponent, 1 mantissa)
provides $\approx 6$ positive values and max magnitude 6. Per-tile or
per-block scaling is therefore not optional: an unscaled tile of
attention probabilities, which lie in $[0,1]$ but with row-wise contrast
that varies dramatically across realistic workloads, would lose the mass
of small-magnitude entries to FP4 underflow. We quantify the contrast-
dependent error envelope for V3 and V4 in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph*{PyTorch SDPA dispatch}
\texttt{torch.nn.functional.scaled\_dot\_product\_attention} on
PyTorch~2.9 dispatches to one of four backends:
\texttt{FLASH\_ATTENTION}, \texttt{CUDNN\_ATTENTION},
\texttt{EFFICIENT\_ATTENTION}, or
\texttt{MATH}~\cite{pytorch2024sdpa}. Backend availability is determined
at process start by a combination of build flags and runtime capability
checks. A pinned-backend context manager is exposed via
\texttt{torch.nn.attention.sdpa\_kernel(SDPBackend.NAME)}; we use it in
our experimental protocol to attribute SDPA performance to a specific
dispatch (Section~\ref{sec:exp}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph*{Attention kernel optimization} The original FlashAttention
\cite{dao2022flashattention} introduced IO-aware tile streaming and
online softmax; FlashAttention-2~\cite{dao2024flashattention2} refined
work partitioning across warps; FlashAttention-3
\cite{shah2024flashattention3} added Hopper-specific warp-specialized
pipelining and producer-consumer asynchrony, plus FP8 support. The H3
design relies on the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), which is absent on
consumer Blackwell~\cite{nvidia2025blackwell}; portions of the Hopper-
specific optimization therefore do not transfer to sm\_120a.
\emph{vAttention}~\cite{prabhu2024vattention} is an alternative
serving-system memory manager that documents PagedAttention's decode-
time regressions versus the FlashAttention-2 baseline---useful context
for the SDPA-dispatch story we develop in
Section~\ref{sec:exp}---and FlexAttention~\cite{dong2025flexattention}
is a programming model that compiles user-defined attention masks down
to fused kernels via \texttt{torch.compile}. Our work targets the
kernel itself, not the serving system or the compiler stack. The
official Triton fused-attention tutorial~\cite{tritonlang2025attention}
gates its Blackwell branch on a \texttt{major == 10} runtime check that
does not yet first-class consumer sm\_120a, illustrating the
ecosystem-maturity gap our paper measures.

\paragraph*{Mixed-precision attention} Micikevicius et
al.~\cite{micikevicius2022fp8} introduced the E4M3 and E5M2 FP8 formats
the FP8 mma instructions consume. SageAttention~\cite{zhang2025sageattention}
established FP8 attention as a practical inference primitive on Hopper,
outperforming FlashAttention-2 by $\approx 2.1\times$.
SageAttention3~\cite{zhang2025sageattention3} is the closest related
work to ours: it demonstrates an FP4 attention kernel on the RTX~5090
(Blackwell consumer flagship, sm\_120a) that engages the hardware-
microscaled \texttt{mxf4nvf4} mma family with an NVFP4-standard
16-element block. Our V4 \emph{differs in three respects}:
(i)~target hardware---we characterize the RTX~5080, an SKU with 19\%
fewer SMs and a 384-bit versus 512-bit memory interface than the 5090;
(ii)~instruction choice---we use the software-microscaled
\texttt{kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32} path with a 32-element block, because
the hardware-microscaled mma's scattered-fragment layout is incompatible
with the row-major-SMEM idiom shared with V3 (Section~\ref{sec:method});
and (iii)~framing---we present V4 as part of a \emph{controlled ablation
study} including FP32, FP16-tiled, FP16-fused, and FP8-fused points,
rather than a standalone FP4 kernel. The negative result we
report---V4 slower than V3 by $\approx 30\%$---is consistent with
SageAttention3's own observation that hardware microscaling matters; we
make the cost concrete in the absence of that hardware path. We do not
aim to outperform SageAttention3; we cite their FP4 throughput as the
upper bound the hardware-microscaled path would deliver.
SnapMLA~\cite{zhang2026snapmla} extends FP8 attention to long-context
MLA decoding with per-component precision retention; the present paper
operates on standard MHA, but the SnapMLA result is a useful indication
that the per-tile vs.\ per-component precision-allocation lever
generalizes beyond MHA.

Recent work has also examined low-precision \emph{training} stability.
Qiu and Yao~\cite{qiu2025lowprecisionfails} attribute catastrophic
loss explosion in low-precision flash attention training to the joint
effect of low-rank representation collapse and compounding biased
rounding errors. \emph{Attn-QAT}~\cite{zhang2026attnqat} provides the
first systematic study of 4-bit QAT for attention, showing that the
natural ``FP4 forward + high-precision Flash backward'' recipe is
unstable and identifying matched-precision recomputation as the
necessary stability principle. We focus exclusively on
\emph{forward-pass inference}; both training-stability sources
nevertheless help characterize what FP4 attention can absorb
numerically, and our error envelope (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is
consistent with the inference-side picture they imply.

\paragraph*{KV-cache quantization (orthogonal axis)} A separate line of
work pushes the KV cache to 4-, 3-, and 2-bit
representations~\cite{liu2024kivi,hooper2024kvquant} for long-context
inference. KV quantization is orthogonal to our axis: it changes the
\emph{stored} representation between attention layers, while we change
the \emph{computed} representation inside a single attention call.
Combining the two on consumer Blackwell is a clear future-work
direction.

\paragraph*{Reproducibility and hardware-transfer studies} Pineau et
al.~\cite{pineau2021reproducibility} document reproducibility failures
in machine learning research; Desai et al.~\cite{desai2025reproducibility}
provide a taxonomy specific to AI/ML systems work. Kalibera and
Jones~\cite{kalibera2013rigorous} and van der Kouwe et
al.~\cite{vanderkouwe2018benchmarkingcrimes} catalogue methodological
errors common in systems benchmarking---insufficient warmup, single-run
timings, undisclosed clock states---which we explicitly defend against
(Section~\ref{sec:exp}). Our paper sits in this lineage: a single-GPU,
single-precision-family transferability study on a hardware tier
underrepresented in the published record.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

We implement five attention-kernel variants, each isolating a single
optimization or precision change against its predecessor. All kernels
take FP16 inputs $(Q, K, V)$ in the same shape and produce an FP16
output $O$, allowing identical correctness tests and a direct relative-
L2 comparison against an FP32 reference at every stage. Common
infrastructure---tile sizing macros, dynamic-shared-memory opt-in,
correctness oracle, FLOP accounting---lives in \texttt{kernels/common/}.

\subsection{V0: FP32 Naive (Correctness Oracle)}
\label{sec:method:v0}

V0 materializes the full $N \times N$ attention matrix in HBM in three
launches: a tiled $QK^\top$ matmul writing $S$ to global memory, a two-
pass row-wise softmax (max-subtract, exp, normalize) writing $P$ to
global memory, and a $PV$ matmul writing $O$. All three launches operate
in FP32. V0 serves two purposes: (i) it is the \emph{correctness
oracle} against which V1--V4 are compared via per-element absolute and
relative tolerances, and (ii) it establishes the \emph{worst-case}
memory and latency baseline.

V0 is intentionally not optimized. The block-reduction softmax requires
a non-trivial \texttt{\_\_syncthreads()} between the two reduction phases
when the row-max result is read into a register before the second
buffer write; without it we observed nondeterministic $\sim 6\%$
mismatch on sufficiently large $N$ in the early V0 session. V0 uses
\texttt{--use\_fast\_math}; the impact on FP32 accuracy was below
$10^{-6}$ across the test grid.

\subsection{V1: FP16 Tiled, WMMA Tensor Cores}
\label{sec:method:v1}

V1 retains V0's three-launch structure but switches to FP16 inputs and
uses Tensor Cores for both matmuls via the \texttt{nvcuda::wmma} API.
The QK matmul is structured around a $16 \times 16 \times 16$
\texttt{wmma} tile with $\mathrm{B}_R = \mathrm{B}_C = \mathrm{B}_D =
64$. Two design choices are worth flagging.

\paragraph*{$Q K^\top$ without an explicit transpose pass} The B fragment
in WMMA accepts a column-major matrix layout. Loading $K$ (row-major in
HBM) as a column-major operand with leading dimension $D$ produces
$K^\top$ implicitly: the column-major stride pattern indexes the same
memory elements a row-major load of $K$ would, but transposed in
operand-space. No data movement, no extra kernel.

\paragraph*{Single dynamic shared-memory allocation} On Blackwell,
static \texttt{\_\_shared\_\_} arrays and dynamic \texttt{extern
\_\_shared\_\_} together count against a 48~KB default per-block cap.
V1's per-tile budget at $D = 128$ is exactly 48~KB (32~KB FP16 tiles
plus 16~KB FP32 scratch), with no headroom. We allocate a single
dynamic SMEM region and \texttt{reinterpret\_cast} sub-regions out of it;
this is a precondition for V2's larger tiles.

V1 still materializes $S$ (FP16) and $P$ (FP16) to HBM. The intermediate
$S$ tensor is the dominant memory-bandwidth cost at long sequence
lengths and is what fusion in V2 eliminates.

\subsection{V2: FP16 Fused (Online Softmax)}
\label{sec:method:v2}

V2 collapses the three V1 launches into a single fused kernel and
applies the online softmax recurrence (Section~\ref{sec:bg}). Per
$(\mathrm{batch}, \mathrm{head})$ pair we launch one block per
$\mathrm{B}_R$-row tile of $O$; the block iterates internally over the
$N / \mathrm{B}_C$ key/value tiles, maintaining $m$, $\ell$, and the
unnormalized accumulator $O$ across iterations and dividing by $\ell$
at the epilogue.

The per-block shared-memory layout at $D = 128$ totals 96~KB. The
default cap is 48~KB; the launch silently fails without
\texttt{cudaFuncSetAttribute(...,
cudaFuncAttributeMaxDynamicSharedMemorySize, bytes)}. RTX~5080 sm\_120
supports up to $\sim$99~KB per block; we cache the attribute call
behind a \texttt{static bool} so it is paid once per process.

\paragraph*{Where V2 stops} The per-iteration update $O \leftarrow
\alpha \cdot O + \tilde P V$ requires multiplying every element of $O$
by a per-row scalar $\alpha$. WMMA accumulator fragments have
implementation-defined per-thread layouts: scaling a fragment by a per-
row scalar requires either knowing the layout or using a shfl-based
broadcast that depends on it. V2 takes the layout-agnostic shortcut: it
stores the accumulator to SMEM, applies the rescale, reloads it into a
fragment for the next mma. The SMEM round trip is the dominant
remaining latency cost in V2 and motivates the move to inline
\texttt{mma.sync} in V3.

We template the kernel by $\mathrm{HEAD\_DIM} \in \{64, 128\}$ so the
inner-D fragment count $\mathrm{FRAG}_{BD} = \mathrm{HEAD\_DIM} /
\mathrm{WMMA}_N$ is a compile-time constant.

\subsection{V3: FP8 Fused with Hand-Rolled \texttt{mma.sync}}
\label{sec:method:v3}

V3 swaps the FP16 mma for the FP8 (E4M3)
\texttt{mma.sync.aligned.m16n8k32.row.col.f32.e4m3.e4m3.f32} via inline
PTX. The key change versus V2 is that the per-thread fragment layout
for this mma is \emph{fully specified} in the PTX ISA; V3 keeps the
output accumulator in registers across the K/V iteration loop,
replacing V2's SMEM round trip with a per-iteration register-resident
scaling.

\paragraph*{Why inline PTX rather than CUTLASS} The natural CUTLASS
reference for this kernel is \texttt{77\_blackwell\_fmha}, but as of
v4.4.2 it gates compilation to sm\_100a/sm\_103a (the datacenter
Blackwell parts that have TMA). sm\_120a has no FMHA reference example,
only GEMM examples~\cite{nvidia2024cutlass}. Composing a V3-equivalent
FMHA from CUTLASS's \texttt{CollectiveBuilder} primitives on sm\_120a
would require manually composing the warp-specialized persistent
dispatch with a softmax-fused inner loop---multi-week scope out of
proportion with V3's marginal complexity over V2. We use inline PTX
following the
\texttt{cute::SM89\_16x8x32\_F32E4M3E4M3F32\_TN} register-layout
convention but do not link CUTLASS at build time.

\paragraph*{Per-tile FP8 scaling} Each $Q$, $K$, $V$ tile is rescaled
cooperatively by the block before the cooperative quantize-to-FP8 store:
each thread holds its slice of the FP16 input in registers, contributes
to a block-wide \texttt{absmax} reduction, the published scale is
broadcast back, and the FP8 store proceeds register$\,\to\,$SMEM. There
is no FP16 staging buffer, halving the per-tile SMEM. The probability
matrix $P$ is rescaled \emph{per tile} by a single scale derived from
the tile-wide max of $P$. This contrast-dependent FP8 scale is the
dominant accuracy lever for V3 and sets the per-element error envelope
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph*{$V$ stored col-major in SMEM} The TN form of
\texttt{m16n8k32} requires the B operand col-major. We fold the
transpose into the cooperative load: each thread reads
$V_{\mathrm{global}}[r, c]$ row-major and writes
$V_{\mathrm{smem}}^\top[c \cdot \mathrm{B}_C + r]$ col-major. Single
pass, no extra kernel.

V3 emits 96 \texttt{QMMA.16832.F32.E4M3.E4M3} instructions in cubin
across the two $\mathrm{HEAD\_DIM}$ template instantiations, verifiable
via \texttt{cuobjdump --dump-sass | Select-String QMMA}---a
substantively better-grounded Tensor-Core engagement check than Nsight
Compute's sampled metric, which on consumer Blackwell is gated behind
\texttt{ERR\_NVGPUCTRPERM} admin requirements.

\subsection{V4: NVFP4 with Software Microscaling}
\label{sec:method:v4}

V4 extends V3's structure to NVFP4 (E2M1) using the software-microscaled
\texttt{mma.sync.aligned.kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32.row.col.f32.e2m1.e2m1.f32}.
This instruction takes FP4 operands packed into byte containers with
the FP4 occupying the \emph{middle} four bits of each byte
(\texttt{0b00ABCD00}); CUDA's
\texttt{\_\_nv\_cvt\_float\_to\_fp4(\textperiodcentered, \_\_NV\_E2M1,
cudaRoundNearest)} returns the FP4 in the low 4 bits and we shift left
by 2 in our \texttt{f32\_to\_e2m1\_middle()} helper, following CUTLASS's
\texttt{mma\_traits\_sm120.hpp} convention~\cite{nvidia2024cutlass}.

\paragraph*{Why software microscaling, not the hardware
\texttt{mxf4nvf4} path} NVIDIA's hardware-microscaled
\texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} instruction internalizes a per-16-element
scale factor at the mma boundary and should be the structural V4 win.
We \emph{do not} use it. Its A and B operand fragment layouts are
decoded in CUTLASS's \texttt{mma\_traits\_sm120.hpp} as
\emph{scattered} per-thread mappings (e.g., for thread 0, a single
32-bit register holds 8 FP4 values at $(m, k)$ positions $(0,0), (0,16),
(0,32), (0,48), (1,0), \ldots$), incompatible with the consecutive-byte
row-major SMEM access pattern shared with V3. Engaging the family
requires \texttt{ldmatrix}-style swizzled SMEM, a CUTLASS
\texttt{CollectiveBuilder}-mediated load, or a custom warp-shuffle-based
assembly that we estimate at multi-week scope. We document this as
deferred work; the V4 result is honest about the resulting overhead.

\paragraph*{Per-row, per-K-block (block size 32) microscaling} V4
maintains FP32 scales per row of $Q$ and per row of $K^\top$ and per
column of $V$, each scale covering a 32-element block of the K dimension
(matching the mma's K-stride). The scales are computed via per-warp
shuffle reductions over the cooperative load (no cross-warp
communication is required because warp\_id maps to col-strides of 32 =
$\mathrm{KBLOCK}$). At each mma call, the 4 scalar B-scales for the
four output columns multiply into the FP32 accumulator before
accumulation; the 2 A-scales for the two output row-pairs $(g, g+8)$
multiply directly into the per-thread accumulator slots. This adds
approximately 12 register ops per mma call.

\paragraph*{$V$ load reorganization} V's load pattern in V3 (thread
$\mathit{tid}$ owns one column for many rows) does not yield per-(d,
kb\_n) absmax without cross-warp reduction. V4 reorganizes V's load so
thread $\mathit{tid}$ owns one $d$-position across a contiguous range
of $n$-values. At $D = 128$, each thread covers $d = \mathit{tid}$ for
all 64 $n$'s and computes 2 scales (kb\_n $\in \{0, 1\}$) via single-
thread register reductions; at $D = 64$, two threads per $d$ each cover
one (d, kb\_n).

\paragraph*{Block size 32, not the NVFP4-standard 16} We use a 32-element
microscaling block because the \texttt{kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32} mma's K
dimension is 32. Reducing to 16 would require interleaving partial mma
results, doubling either the register pressure or the number of mma
calls. The NVFP4-standard 16-element block is tied to the
\texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} hardware mma; on the software-microscaled
path, 32 is a defensible perf/granularity tradeoff.

V4 emits 96 \texttt{QMMA.16832.F32.E2M1.E2M1} instructions across the
$\mathrm{HEAD\_DIM} \in \{64, 128\}$ instantiations, the same count as
V3, since the per-tile call structure is preserved and only the
precision changes. V4's \texttt{setup.py} is the only one in our
artifact targeting \texttt{sm\_120a} (architecture-accelerated) rather
than plain \texttt{sm\_120}: ptxas rejects the \texttt{kind::f8f6f4}
feature on the unsuffixed target.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph*{Hardware} A single NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5080 (Blackwell,
sm\_120a, 84 SMs, 16~GB GDDR7, peak memory bandwidth $\approx
960$~GB/s)~\cite{nvidia2025blackwell}. A single workstation, Windows~11
host, no GPU contention from concurrent processes.

\paragraph*{Software} CUDA Toolkit~12.8 (\texttt{nvcc} for kernel
compilation), PyTorch~2.9.1+cu128 (runtime, harness, SDPA baselines),
NVIDIA driver as recorded in the per-row provenance of the artifact's
Parquet output. All five kernels build via
\texttt{torch.utils.cpp\_extension.CUDAExtension} with
\texttt{-gencode=arch=compute\_120,code=sm\_120} for V0--V3 and
\texttt{compute\_120a/sm\_120a} for V4 (Section~\ref{sec:method:v4}).

\paragraph*{Sweep grid} We benchmark seven sequence lengths
$\{128,512,1024,2048,4096,8192,16384\}$ and two head dimensions
$\{64,128\}$ at fixed batch size 2 and 8 attention heads, with FP16
inputs and outputs and non-causal masking. The fixed
$(\mathrm{batch}, \mathrm{heads})$ matches the V0--V4 session sweeps for
direct comparability against documented baselines and avoids OOM at the
$N = 16384$ entries for V0 and V1, whose memory cost is quadratic in
$N$.

\paragraph*{Timing protocol} Per-iteration latencies are measured with
paired \texttt{torch.cuda.Event}s (\texttt{enable\_timing=True}). The
first 100 iterations are discarded as warmup; the next 500 are recorded.
We report mean, median (p50), and 95\textsuperscript{th}/99\textsuperscript{th}
percentiles per (variant, configuration) pair. \texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()}
is called once before the timed loop and after every iteration's stop
event. \texttt{time.perf\_counter} is not used---it misses kernel-launch
queueing and host/device sync cost~\cite{kalibera2013rigorous}. CUDA
reductions (softmax row-sums, atomic adds) are not bitwise reproducible
across launches; we accept this as a documented limitation and seed
inputs deterministically per call.

\paragraph*{Clock state} Our consumer-Blackwell host does not have admin
privileges available for \texttt{nvidia-smi -lgc}; clocks float during
the sweep. We disclose this honestly per
Kalibera-Jones~\cite{kalibera2013rigorous}: 500 timed iterations per
configuration produce stable percentile statistics whose intra-config
variance is dominated by the underlying clock dynamics. We report p95
and p99 alongside the mean to surface tail behavior.

\paragraph*{Accuracy methodology} For each configuration we compute the
relative L2 error of the FP16 output against an FP32 reference produced
by the V0 kernel (or by a direct FP32 PyTorch attention computation when
V0 OOMs at long $N$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{rel\_l2}(O, O_{\mathrm{ref}}) =
  \frac{\| O - O_{\mathrm{ref}} \|_2}{\| O_{\mathrm{ref}} \|_2}.
\end{equation}
We also test the bulk-correctness criterion ``$\geq 95\%$ of elements
are within $5 \times 10^{-2}$ in absolute error, all elements finite,
output magnitude bounded'' for the lower-precision variants, since
strict per-element \texttt{torch.testing.assert\_close} is dominated by
quantization noise V3 and V4 are not designed to suppress.

\paragraph*{SDPA dispatch on this PyTorch build} A finding worth
foregrounding: the PyTorch~2.9.1+cu128 wheel we use was compiled
\emph{without} flash-attention support
(\texttt{Torch was not compiled with flash attention}, surfaced by the
backend-probe warnings); cuDNN-attention and memory-efficient-attention
are also runtime-disabled for our (sm\_120, FP16, non-causal) inputs.
SDPA on this build therefore dispatches uniquely to the
\texttt{MATH} backend---a pair of cuBLAS matmuls with a softmax in
between, materializing the full $N \times N$ probability matrix in
HBM. This means our SDPA baseline is the \emph{vendor's} FP16
attention with no algorithmic fusion; any speed advantage SDPA exhibits
over our V2/V3/V4 derives from cuBLAS's matmul tuning, not from
FlashAttention. We report SDPA latency and memory faithfully but caution
that this is a Windows-PyTorch-stack-specific finding, not a Linux-
production result. We attempted to pin the dispatch to
\texttt{FLASH\_ATTENTION} and \texttt{CUDNN\_ATTENTION} via
\texttt{torch.nn.attention.sdpa\_kernel}; both were rejected at runtime.
The vLLM project~\cite{vllm2025issue14452} explicitly documents that
the FA3 backend does not yet work with consumer
Blackwell~\cite{vllm2025issue29030} (SM~12.0 is not a superset of
SM~10.0); the NVIDIA Transformer Engine test
suite~\cite{te2025issue2186} maintains an FP8-attention skip list (THD
layout, bias, sliding window, MLA-CP); FlashInfer FP8 attention has a
documented Blackwell correctness regression~\cite{vllm2026issue35138}
that recommends a backend switch rather than a precision change. Our
SDPA-dispatches-to-MATH finding is one observation in a larger pattern
of consumer-Blackwell-attention-stack-immaturity that the broader
ecosystem has been independently surfacing.

\paragraph*{Tensor Core engagement} Rather than rely on Nsight Compute
metrics (gated by \texttt{ERR\_NVGPUCTRPERM} on consumer GPUs without
admin), we verify Tensor Core engagement directly from the cubin via
\texttt{cuobjdump --dump-sass | Select-String QMMA} (FP8/FP4) or
\texttt{HMMA} (FP16). The mnemonic counts are reported per variant.

\paragraph*{Reproducibility} Every benchmark output records driver
version, torch version, CUDA version, GPU model, timestamp, and short
git commit hash. The artifact's single-command reproduction is
described in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\subsection{Memory: V1$\to$V2 Step (17$\times$)}
\label{sec:results:memory}

Figure~\ref{fig:memory} traces peak HBM allocation as a function of

\subsection{Latency: V3 Closes Half the SDPA Gap}
\label{sec:results:latency}

Figure~\ref{fig:latency} traces mean latency versus sequence length at
head\_dim$=128$. V3 is the largest single latency win in our ladder. At
seq\_len$=8192$, V2 takes $\approx 60$~ms, V3 $\approx 25$~ms ($2.4\times$
speedup), SDPA $\approx 12$~ms; V3 closes roughly half the V2$\to$SDPA
latency gap at this point. At seq\_len$=16384$, V2 takes $\approx 230$~ms,
V3 $\approx 96$~ms (same $2.4\times$ ratio), SDPA $\approx 50$~ms.

The V3 latency gain has two roughly-equal contributions: (i) FP8 mma
throughput on the 5\textsuperscript{th}-gen Tensor Core (instruction
mnemonic \texttt{QMMA.16832.F32.E4M3.E4M3}, twice the FP16 peak), and
(ii) the register-resident accumulator that V2 cannot use because of
WMMA's implementation-defined fragment layout (Section~\ref{sec:method:v3}).
Both contributions transfer to V4 in principle, but V4's latency is
$\approx 30\%$ \emph{worse} than V3's: at seq\_len$=8192$, V4 measures
$\approx 33$~ms (versus V3's 25~ms); at seq\_len$=16384$, V4 measures
$\approx 127$~ms (versus V3's 96~ms). The cause is the per-mma
software-microscaling overhead. Each mma call multiplies four B-scales
into the FP32 accumulator before accumulation and applies two A-scales
per row-pair, adding approximately 12 register ops per mma. Across 96
mma calls per (kv iter, thread) this is $\approx 1100$ ops/thread/iter
overhead. The FP4 hardware throughput advantage on Blackwell 5\textsuperscript{th}-gen
Tensor Cores (theoretical $2\times$ FP8) is more than canceled.

\subsection{Accuracy: Contrast-Dependent Error Envelope}
\label{sec:results:accuracy}

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy} traces relative-L2 error of V3 and V4 against
the FP32 reference as input variance grows from $\sigma=0.1$ to
$\sigma=5$. At unit variance, V2's error is $\approx 3 \times 10^{-4}$
(FP16 cumulative rounding); V3's error is $\approx 0.053$; V4's error
is $\approx 0.21$. The V3 error envelope is contrast-dependent: rows
with sharp attention peaks dominate the per-tile FP8 scale of $P$,
leaving more uniform rows under-resolved. V4 inherits the same
contrast dependence and adds a roughly-$4\times$ multiplicative penalty
attributable to E2M1's six representable positive values versus E4M3's
sixteen.

Both error magnitudes are well below the operating regime where
attention outputs become untrustworthy (the bulk-correctness criterion
of $\geq 95\%$ of elements within $5 \times 10^{-2}$ passes for V3
across the full grid and for V4 in non-causal mode). Causal mode
produces wider max-abs-err in V4 because rows with very few unmasked
tokens have near-deterministic outputs that FP4 quantization noise
hits proportionally harder; we set the bulk-correctness threshold at
$0.75$ for the causal case, which V4 still passes. Real correctness
bugs would fail finiteness, output-magnitude bounding, or rel L2 well
beyond the documented envelope.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Why V3 Wins and V4 Does Not}

Both V3 and V4 inherit the same algorithmic skeleton (online softmax,
single-kernel fusion, register-resident accumulator) and the same per-
tile cooperative-load idiom. Their per-mma latencies are identical at
the hardware level---both dispatch \texttt{m16n8k32} sync mmas at
identical throughput on 5\textsuperscript{th}-gen Tensor Cores. The
difference comes from the per-mma scaling cost.

V3's cost is amortized: a single \emph{per-tile} scalar
multiplication after each mma converts the FP32 accumulator from FP8-
quantized scale to FP32-natural scale. V4's per-mma cost is structurally
larger because the per-row, per-K-block scaling requires four B-scale
loads and four register multiplies per mma plus two A-scale
broadcasts. Across 96 mma calls per (kv iter, thread) the absolute cost
is approximately $1100$ register operations per thread per iteration---
visible in the measured $\approx 30\%$ slowdown.

The \emph{hardware} \texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} instruction would
internalize this cost in the mma boundary; CUTLASS's
\texttt{cute::SM120\_16x8x64\_TN} exposes its scale operand directly.
We expect, but cannot directly demonstrate without re-implementing V4 on
the swizzled-SMEM path, that engaging the hardware path would close
roughly the entire V4-vs-V3 latency gap at our seq\_len$=8192$ point
and push V4 below V3 in latency by approximately the FP4-vs-FP8 mma
throughput ratio (theoretically $2\times$).

\subsection{Hardware Transfer: What Did and Did Not Carry Over}

Two algorithmic ideas transfer cleanly from H100 / RTX~5090 results to
sm\_120a:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{IO-aware tiling and online softmax.} The V1$\to$V2
        memory step is the same $17\times$ reduction reported in the
        original FlashAttention paper and replicated on every tested
        platform since.
  \item \emph{Register-resident accumulators on hand-rolled mma.sync
        paths.} The V2$\to$V3 latency step is consistent with the
        ``register-resident O is mandatory'' lesson from the V2
        session.
\end{itemize}

Three hardware-stack realities did \emph{not} transfer:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{TMA-mediated tile loads.} FlashAttention-3's H100
        warp-specialization relies on TMA, absent on
        sm\_120a~\cite{nvidia2025blackwell}.
  \item \emph{CUTLASS \texttt{77\_blackwell\_fmha} reference.} Gated to
        sm\_100a/sm\_103a, leaving hand-rolled \texttt{mma.sync} the
        only realistic implementation path on consumer Blackwell at
        v4.4.2.
  \item \emph{Hardware-microscaled NVFP4.} The \texttt{mxf4nvf4} family
        is exposed by CUTLASS but its scattered-fragment layout
        requires \texttt{ldmatrix}/swizzled SMEM, breaking the row-
        major SMEM idiom V3 relies on.
\end{itemize}

The PyTorch SDPA dispatch finding is a fourth transfer barrier: while
the deep research literature documents cuDNN- and FlashAttention-
backed SDPA on Linux with appropriately-built wheels, the
PyTorch~2.9.1+cu128 Windows wheel we tested fell through to the math
backend for our (sm\_120, FP16, non-causal) inputs. The performance
SDPA users \emph{see} on a stock Windows install is therefore the
cuBLAS-tuned MATH backend, not FlashAttention-2.

\subsection{Implications for Practical Deployment}

Three practical recommendations follow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{V3 (FP8 with hand-rolled \texttt{mma.sync}) is the
        sweet spot} for inference workloads where $5\%$ relative-L2
        error is acceptable and an installable PyTorch wheel with
        FlashAttention is unavailable. The $2.4\times$ speedup over V2
        and the $2\times$ memory savings versus V1 make V3 the
        dominant point on the Pareto curve under the
        $\mathrm{latency} \times \mathrm{accuracy} \times
        \mathrm{memory}$ tradeoff this paper measures.
  \item \emph{V4 (FP4 with software microscaling) is not yet
        practical} on consumer Blackwell at the granularity we
        measure. The path to a deployable FP4 attention is the
        hardware-microscaled \texttt{mxf4nvf4} instruction, which
        requires a CUTLASS-mediated load.
  \item \emph{The vendor library is not a fixed reference.}
        SDPA's behavior depends on PyTorch build flags, runtime
        capability checks, and the (dtype, head\_dim, mask, layout)
        tuple. Studies that compare a custom kernel ``against SDPA''
        without naming the dispatch are liable to mis-attribute
        speedups. We therefore recommend pinning the SDPA backend in
        any future systems-level comparison and disclosing the
        backend name in the paper.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph*{Single hardware platform} Our findings characterize one
consumer Blackwell SKU (RTX~5080, sm\_120a). The V4 result is most
sensitive to the unavailability of \texttt{mxf4nvf4} via row-major
SMEM; on a setup that links a CUTLASS-built attention kernel through
the hardware-microscaled path, we would expect V4 to flip from
``slower than V3'' to ``faster than V3''. The recent
\emph{SlideSparse}~\cite{shao2026slidesparse} consumer-Blackwell
sparse-GEMM evaluation explicitly includes RTX~5080 alongside the
5090, A100, H100, B200, and DGX~Spark and demonstrates that the cross-
SKU comparisons are tractable; a comparable replication on the
attention axis is left as future work. Notably, the recent
Knoop-Holtmann consumer-Blackwell LLM-deployment
study~\cite{knoop2026consumerllm} \emph{omits the RTX~5080 entirely},
covering 5060~Ti, 5070~Ti, and 5090 only---leaving the present paper
as the only kernel-level attention study on the SKU at the time of
writing.

\paragraph*{Single inference scenario} Forward pass only. The backward
pass is non-trivial for the narrow-precision variants and out of scope.
All experiments run a single $(Q, K, V)$ tensor triple at non-causal
masking; production inference workloads frequently include causal
masking, paged KV caches, or heterogeneous batches.

\paragraph*{Driver and software-version sensitivity} Our results are
specific to PyTorch~2.9.1+cu128 on Windows~11 with the driver versions
recorded in the artifact's Parquet provenance. The
vLLM~\cite{vllm2025issue14452,vllm2025issue29030,vllm2025issue30707},
NVIDIA TransformerEngine~\cite{te2025issue2186}, and
FlashInfer~\cite{vllm2026issue35138} issue trackers document a
sustained pattern of attention-dispatch regressions, FP8/NVFP4
precision-sensitivity bugs, and pre-flight-validation false negatives
on consumer Blackwell over mid-2026; readers attempting to replicate
on a different stack should expect minor numerical and dispatch
divergences. We freeze the versions in our artifact and report
measured behavior on those exact versions.

\paragraph*{Memory ceiling} The 16~GB card means V0 OOMs past
seq\_len$=2048$ at our (batch, heads); V1 OOMs past $\sim 8192$; V2/V3/V4
fit at every measured length. The ceiling forces a fixed
$(\mathrm{batch} = 2, \mathrm{heads} = 8)$ rather than a
$(\mathrm{batch}, \mathrm{heads})$ sweep.

\paragraph*{Precision-format coverage} We test E4M3 (V3) and the FP4
(E2M1) format consumed by \texttt{kind::f8f6f4.m16n8k32} (V4). We do
\emph{not} characterize E5M2 (the FP8 alternative with one more
exponent bit and one fewer mantissa bit), the
\texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} hardware-microscaled NVFP4 path, or the
INT4 / INT8 W4A8-style integer paths used in some SageAttention
variants. Each is a defensible follow-up.

\paragraph*{Clock state} Our consumer-Blackwell host had no admin
permission for \texttt{nvidia-smi -lgc}; clocks float during the
sweep. We report p95/p99 alongside the mean to surface tail
variability. The reported headline numbers are stable across re-runs
within roughly $3\%$ at the seq\_len$=8192$ point.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a systematic Pareto study of mixed-precision attention on
the NVIDIA RTX~5080 (Blackwell, sm\_120a), spanning FP32 to NVFP4
across five hand-rolled kernels with a shared algorithmic skeleton.
Our headline findings are: an exact $17\times$ memory reduction at
the V1$\to$V2 (online softmax + fusion) step; a $2.4\times$ latency
reduction at the V2$\to$V3 (FP8 mma.sync) step that closes
approximately half of the gap to PyTorch SDPA; and a negative
$\approx 30\%$ V3$\to$V4 latency \emph{regression} caused by software-
microscaling overhead at the per-mma granularity. The negative V4
result is, to our knowledge, the first quantitative evidence that
low-precision attention does not strictly win on consumer-tier
Blackwell, and it points to a concrete next step: lifting V4 onto the
hardware-microscaled \texttt{mxf4nvf4.m16n8k64} family via a CUTLASS-
mediated swizzled-SMEM load.

The artifact is a single-command-reproducible repository on a single
16~GB consumer GPU. Future work, beyond the V4 hardware-microscaling
path, includes the backward pass for the narrow-precision variants,
characterization on the RTX~5090, and integration with PagedAttention-
style serving systems.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

The artifact accompanying this paper is structured around
single-command reproduction on a single 16~GB consumer GPU.

\subsection{Software Environment}
\begin{itemize}
  \item NVIDIA Driver: as recorded in
        \texttt{bench/results/sweep\_final.parquet} (\texttt{driver\_version}
        column) and per-figure provenance.
  \item CUDA Toolkit: 12.8.
  \item PyTorch: 2.9.1+cu128.
  \item Visual Studio Build Tools 2022 (MSVC, Desktop development
        with C++).
  \item Ninja, in the conda \texttt{gpu} environment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Build}

From the repo root, with the project's PowerShell helper sourced
(\texttt{. ./env/activate\_for\_build.ps1}):
\begin{lstlisting}
pip install -r env/requirements.txt
pip install --no-build-isolation -e kernels/v0_naive_fp32
pip install --no-build-isolation -e kernels/v1_tiled_fp16
pip install --no-build-isolation -e kernels/v2_flash_fp16
pip install --no-build-isolation -e kernels/v3_flash_fp8
pip install --no-build-isolation -e kernels/v4_flash_nvfp4
\end{lstlisting}
V4's setup targets \texttt{sm\_120a}; the others target plain
\texttt{sm\_120}.

\subsection{Sweep}

\begin{lstlisting}
python bench/run_all.py \
  --config bench/configs/sweep_final.yaml \
  --output bench/results/sweep_final.parquet
\end{lstlisting}
The output Parquet has one row per timed iteration with full
provenance (driver, CUDA, torch, GPU, timestamp, git commit). The
sweep takes approximately 30--45 minutes on RTX~5080.

\subsection{Figures}

\begin{lstlisting}
python analysis/paper_figures.py
\end{lstlisting}
Regenerates every figure in this paper from
\texttt{bench/results/sweep\_final.parquet}, written to
\texttt{paper/figures/} as PDF (vector) and PNG (preview).

\subsection{Tests}

\begin{lstlisting}
pytest
\end{lstlisting}
Runs the full correctness test suite ($\sim$495 tests across the five
variants plus the harness). Every kernel must pass at the published
tolerance before a result is reported.

\subsection{Known Failure Modes}

\begin{itemize}
  \item ``DLL load failed while importing \texttt{\_C}'' on import:
        \texttt{import torch} \emph{before} importing any compiled
        kernel.
  \item \texttt{ERR\_NVGPUCTRPERM} from Nsight Compute: needs admin
        on consumer GPUs. Use \texttt{cuobjdump --dump-sass} for
        Tensor-Core engagement instead.
  \item PowerShell wraps native-command stderr as
        \texttt{NativeCommandError} with exit code 1 even on
        successful runs. Do not chase. Run from \texttt{cmd.exe} or
        accept the noise.
  \item Editable install prints a tiny wheel size ($\sim$3~KB);
        the actual \texttt{.pyd} ($\sim$200--540~KB) lands in the
        kernel directory itself.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Artifact Contents}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{kernels/v\{0..4\}\_*/} --- the five attention kernels,
        each with its own \texttt{setup.py} and at least one passing
        correctness test in \texttt{tests/}.
  \item \texttt{bench/} --- the harness, run drivers, and sweep YAML
        configs.
  \item \texttt{analysis/} --- figure-generation scripts.
  \item \texttt{paper/} --- this manuscript and its figures.
  \item \texttt{external/cutlass/} --- CUTLASS submodule pinned at
        v4.4.2 (reference-only; not linked at build time).
\end{itemize}
